THE DEMON SAMURAI
Fashion and Mysteries
Hélène Blignaut

LETTER FROM THE AUTHOR TO READERS
Japan in the 16th century,
the dramatic story of Nobunaga Oda, the demon samurai who changed the destiny of a country, a formidable practical intelligence that drove him in a relentless rush forward toward the light of his precise objective, beyond the drowsy acquiescence of mediocrity, the sloth of the incompetent, the violent pettiness of the unrealistic.
He was driven by a constant urge to represent himself and create something that would last forever, like an artist who, from a hypothetical nothingness on which the nothingness of the masses lies inert, extracts the creation of the unexpected, the work of art, that vital transgression whose meaning will multiply itself infinitely.

1

Special thanks go to Father Louis Fròis, Portuguese Jesuit missionary, who lived for a long time in Japan in the second half of the 16th century,
and stayed for some time in a castle owned by Nobunaga Oda. The Jesuit sent his European superiors lengthy letters, a detailed diary in which he described Japanese customs and traditions, reported war chronicles, and painted a portrait of Nobunaga unlike any other, often clearing away the myriad of nebulous information and clichés that have come down to us about his astonishing exploits.

Poetry, in its many genres, has been a hallmark of Japanese history since the earliest centuries, but Japanese poems, like those of every other country in the world, are untranslatable because the poet relies on sound and lexical and stylistic virtuosity to convey the profound meaning.

Hélène Blignaut
